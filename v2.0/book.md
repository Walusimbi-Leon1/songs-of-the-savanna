# Songs of the Savanna

**A Novel by Walusimbi Leon**

---

## Version 2.0 — First Edition

*A twelve-year-old girl in a rural village learns the old songs from her grandmother, and through them inherits a history the schoolbooks erased.*

### Chapter 2 — The Stone That Remembers

The grinding stone sang before Naava did. It was a low, patient groan—river rock against river rock, the sound older than any song, older than the village, older perhaps than the savanna itself. Nankya lay on her mat, one eye open in the gray light before sunrise, watching her grandmother's silhouette bend and straighten, bend and straighten, arms moving in a rhythm that had outlasted kingdoms.

The millet fell like pale rain. Naava's hands turned the top stone, and the whole compound breathed with it: the chickens shifting in their coop, the goat bleating once from its tether, the smoke from the cooking fire curling upward in a question.

Nankya waited. She knew better than to speak first. The songs came when they came, and they came when Naava decided.

The grinding slowed. Naava wiped her brow with the back of her wrist, looked at the sky, and began to hum. The hum was low, almost a growl, and it seemed to come from somewhere deep in the earth rather than from her throat. Nankya sat up slowly, her heart quickening.

*"When the sun was a child,"* Naava sang, her voice cracking into the morning air, *"it walked on two legs. When the sun was a child, it answered to the drum."*

Nankya knew this one. She had heard it a hundred times, but it never settled in her the same way twice. Today it felt different. Today, something in her grandmother's voice had shifted, an urgency underneath the melody like water moving beneath ice.

"Grandmother," Nankya said, the word slipping out before she could stop it.

Naava did not stop grinding. She did not turn. But the hum continued, and after a moment she said, "The song is not finished, child."

"I know. But—"

"Then listen."

Nankya closed her mouth. She pulled her cloth around her shoulders and sat cross-legged on her mat, watching the sun breach the distant acacia line. The grass was tall and gold, swaying in a wind she could not feel. The sky was the color of unripe mangoes. And her grandmother's voice folded into all of it, weaving itself into the fabric of the morning.

*"The sun walked with the first people. It walked with the first cows. It stood at the door of the first house and asked for water. And the first mother, she gave it milk. The first father, he gave it fire. The sun remembered. The sun remembers."*

Naava's hands stopped moving. The stone grew quiet. In the silence, Nankya heard the flies buzzing around the goat pen, heard her own breath, heard the far-off call of a hornbill.

"That is the song," Naava said, settling onto a low stool beside the grinding stone. She was not a tall woman, but sitting she seemed to take up more space than her body warranted. Her face was a map of lines—some deep, some shallow, all of them pointing toward eyes that missed nothing.

"But where was the sun going?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. It was a thin smile, barely a curve, but it warmed her whole face. "You ask the right question. Your mother never asked the right question. Neither did your father."

"Where was it going?"

"The sun was going home. But the sun had forgotten where home was. So the people sang it back. Every day, they sang the sun back across the sky. And that is why we sing, child. We are the ones who remember. We are the ones who sing things back to where they belong."

Nankya turned this over in her mind. It was like a stone in her palm, smooth and heavy, with hidden edges. "But the teacher says the sun is a star. A ball of fire in the sky. It doesn't have legs."

Naava laughed. It was a sharp, sudden sound, like a bird taking flight. "The teacher says many things. Some of them are true. Some of them are only true in the world the teacher lives in."

"Which world do we live in?"

Naava leaned forward. Her eyes, dark and steady, held Nankya's gaze. "We live in the world that was here before the teacher's world. We live in the world of the songs. And in that world, the sun walks, and the sun remembers, and the sun asks for water at the door of the first house. You will learn the teacher's world in the classroom. But you must learn our world here. Otherwise, you will be a person with only one eye, and one eye is not enough to see the whole sky."

Nankya felt a shiver run up her spine. It was not a cold shiver. It was the feeling of a door opening inside her, a door she had not known was there.

"Will you teach me?" she asked.

Naava stood. She walked to the cooking fire and stirred the pot of porridge that had been simmering. The steam rose, carrying the smell of maize and a hint of sweetness. "I am already teaching you," she said. "Every morning, you sit here. Every morning, you listen. That is how it is done. That is how it has always been done."

"But the songs—they're so many. How will I remember them all?"

"You will not remember them all." Naava's voice was matter-of-fact, without comfort. "No one remembers them all. The songs are not yours to remember. They are yours to carry for a while, and then to pass on."

"To who?"

Naava was quiet for a long moment. She ladled porridge into a clay bowl and handed it to Nankya. The bowl was warm in her hands, the porridge thick and smooth. "You will know," Naava said. "When the time comes, you will know. The song will tell you."

Nankya ate her porridge in silence, watching her grandmother move through the compound. Naava's movements were economical, precise—she wasted nothing, not in her body, not in her words. Everything she did had purpose. Everything she said had weight.

When the bowl was empty, Nankya set it down and asked the question she had been carrying all morning.

"Grandmother, what happened to the songs?"

Naava's back was to her. She was folding a cloth, her hands moving slowly, deliberately. "What do you mean?"

"The teacher said our songs are not real. He said they are stories that people made up because they did not know science. He said the real history is in the books."

Naava finished folding the cloth. She placed it on the ground, smoothed it once with her palm, and then turned to face Nankya. Her face was unreadable, but her voice was soft, softer than Nankya had ever heard it.

"The books," Naava said, "are written by people who were not there. The songs are sung by people who were. Which do you think is more real?"

"The songs?"

"The songs." Naava nodded. "But you must be careful, child. The books are strong. They are everywhere. They will tell you that you are small, that your people are small, that your history is small. They will tell you that the songs are lies. And if you are not careful, you will believe them. Because the books are loud, and the songs are quiet."

"Then how do I keep the songs loud in my head?"

Naava walked to Nankya and knelt in front of her. She took Nankya's hands in her own, and Nankya felt the roughness of her palms, the calluses from years of work. "You sing them," she said. "You sing them every day. You sing them when you are happy, and you sing them when you are sad. You sing them when you are alone, and you sing them when you are with others. You sing them until they are not in your head anymore, but in your bones. And then, when the books are loud, you will not hear them. You will only hear the songs."

Nankya looked at her grandmother's face. In the morning light, the lines seemed less deep, the eyes brighter. There was something ancient in Naava's gaze, something that had seen too much and yet kept seeing.

"Will you sing with me?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. This time, the smile was full. "Yes," she said. "I will sing with you. But first, you must learn to listen."

She stood and walked to the eastern edge of the compound, where the baobab tree stood sentinel. Its trunk was massive, gnarled, older than memory. Naava placed her palm against the bark and closed her eyes.

"Listen," she said.

Nankya walked to her grandmother's side and placed her own hand on the tree. The bark was rough, warm from the rising sun. She closed her eyes.

"What am I listening for?"

"The tree," Naava said. "The tree is singing. Can you hear it?"

Nankya listened. At first, she heard only the wind, the distant birds, the low rumble of a truck on the road far away. But then, beneath those sounds, she heard something else. A vibration. A hum. It was faint, almost imperceptible, but it was there, rising up from the roots through the trunk and into her palm.

"I hear it," she whispered.

Naava opened her eyes. "Good," she said. "Now you are ready."

The sun climbed higher. The compound filled with the sounds of the day—the clatter of pots, the laughter of children, the lowing of cattle being led to pasture. But Nankya stood at the baobab with her hand on the bark, listening. And in her chest, the song took root.

### Chapter 3 — The Song of the Long Water

The moon was still high when Naava's voice threaded through the compound, thin and certain, drawing Nankya from sleep. She lay on her mat for a moment, the song curling around her like smoke, and then she rose. No lamp, no lantern—the dark was a familiar garment, worn easily. She stepped over her sleeping brothers, careful where she placed her feet, and went out into the open air.

Naava was already at the grinding stone, her body bent over it in a silhouette the darkness could not fully claim. The song she sang was old—Nankya could tell by the shape of it, the way the words did not fit the mouth easily, the way they seemed to come from someplace deeper than breath. Naava's arms moved in a steady rhythm, the stone groaning against the millet, and the song moved with her, rising and falling like water finding its level.

*Kuno, kuno, amazzi gajja,*
*Kuno, kuno, gajja mu ngalo…*

Nankya sat on the packed earth near her grandmother's feet and listened. The words were familiar, but only in the way a dream is familiar—you know you have seen it before, but you cannot say where or when. She had heard this song many times, but this morning it seemed different. This morning, the words seemed to be reaching for something.

"Grandmother," she said, when the song paused for breath, "what is that song about?"

Naava did not stop grinding. "It is the song of the long water. The water that came from the sky and stayed. The water that gave us life when the land was dying."

"What long water?"

Naava's hands stilled. She looked at Nankya, and in the half-light of dawn, her eyes were two dark pools. "You have seen the lake? The one the road passes?"

Nankya nodded. She had been there once, with her father, when he had gone to sell goats at the trading post. The lake had stretched to the horizon, a sheet of silver under the sun, and she had stood at its edge and felt very small.

"That lake," Naava said, "was not always there. There was a time, long ago, when this land was dry. The rivers were dust. The animals walked until they fell. The people looked at the sky and saw only fire."

"What happened?"

Naava resumed her grinding. The stone sang its rough song. "There was a girl," she said. "A girl not much older than you. Her name was Nalwoga. She was the daughter of a chief, but she had no special power, no special gift. She was just a girl who loved her people. And when the land dried, and the people began to die, she could not bear it."

"What did she do?"

"She walked. She walked to the east, where the sun rises, because the elders said that is where the sky keeps its water. She walked for many days. Her feet cracked. Her throat closed. But she walked. And when she reached the edge of the world, where the sky meets the earth, she found a door."

"A door?"

"A door made of rain. It was locked, but it was not locked with iron. It was locked with silence." Naava's voice dropped, and the grinding slowed. "So Nalwoga sang. She sang the song of her people, the song of the land, the song of her mother and her mother's mother. She sang until her voice was raw, until her throat was blood, until the silence broke and the door opened."

"And then?"

"Then the water came. It came from the door, a great river of it, and it followed her home. It filled the dry riverbeds and the cracked earth. It filled the hollow of the land, where the lake is now. And the people drank, and the animals drank, and the land lived again."

Nankya sat very still. The story sat in her chest like a warm stone. "Is that true?"

Naava's hands stopped. She looked at Nankya for a long moment. "Is what true? That a girl walked to the edge of the world? That she sang a song that opened a door made of rain?"

"Yes. All of it."

Naava wiped her hands on her apron. "The lake is there," she said. "The water is there. The song is there. That is what I know. The rest—" She shrugged. "The teacher would say it is a story. But the lake does not care what the teacher says. The lake is full. The lake is there."

Nankya turned the words over in her mind. She had seen the lake. She had stood at its edge and felt the damp wind on her face. She had seen the fishermen on its surface, their canoes like water bugs. The lake was real. The lake was enormous. And now her grandmother was telling her that it had been sung into being by a girl named Nalwoga.

"Was Nalwoga real?" she asked.

Naava tilted her head. "Was she real? Your mother is real. Your father is real. Your brothers are real. But where are they now?"

"At home. In the village."

"And where is the village?"

"Here."

"And where is here?"

Nankya frowned. "I don't understand."

Naava stood and walked to the cooking fire. She stirred the embers, blew on them, coaxed a small flame to life. "The teacher would say that the village is at a certain latitude and longitude. He would say it is on a map, between this road and that road. He would say it has a certain number of people and a certain number of houses. All of that is true. But is that what makes the village real?"

Nankya shook her head slowly.

"No," Naava said. "The village is real because your mother was born here. Because your grandfather is buried here. Because the first tree was planted here by a woman whose name we no longer remember, but whose hands we still use. The village is real because the songs are sung here. And the songs are real because they are sung. That is what makes something real, child. Not whether it happened. Whether it is remembered."

"But the teacher said—"

"The teacher is not here." Naava's voice was firm, but not unkind. "The teacher is in his classroom, teaching his books. But you are here. And I am here. And the sun is rising, and the millet is ground, and we are alive. That is the truth I know."

Nankya was quiet for a long moment. The fire crackled. The first birds began to call, their voices threading through the gray light. She thought about Nalwoga walking to the edge of the world. She thought about the door made of rain. She thought about the lake, vast and silver, and how she had stood at its edge and felt it pulling at her, as if it recognized her.

"Grandmother," she said, "will you teach me the song of the long water?"

Naava smiled. It was a slow smile, spreading across her face like water finding its level. "That is a long song," she said. "It takes many days to learn. And it is not a song you can rush. It is a song you must grow into."

"I want to grow into it."

Naava looked at her granddaughter, and in her eyes, Nankya saw something shift. A door opening. A recognition. "You have your grandmother's stubbornness," Naava said. "And your mother's eyes. And your father's heart." She reached out and touched Nankya's cheek with her rough palm. "The song will come. But first, we must build your bones. A song like that cannot live in a weak body. It needs a strong house."

"What do I have to do?"

"You will do what I do. Every morning, you will grind the millet. You will fetch the water. You will tend the fire. You will learn the work of the body, so that the body can carry the work of the song." Naava's voice softened. "The songs do not live in the voice alone, child. They live in the hands, and the feet, and the back. They live in the way you stand and the way you walk. A song is not only sung. It is lived."

Nankya stood. She walked to the grinding stone and placed her hands on the smooth, worn surface. It was warm from Naava's work, and she could feel the ghost of the motion, the rhythm her grandmother had pressed into it over the years. She picked up the smaller stone and began to grind, her movements clumsy at first, the millet slipping and scattering.

"Like this," Naava said, and she knelt beside Nankya, placing her hands over Nankya's, guiding the motion. "Slowly. The stone is not in a hurry. The millet is not in a hurry. You are not in a hurry."

Nankya let her grandmother's hands move hers. The rhythm was strange, but she could feel it taking shape, the circle of it, the patience of it. The millet began to crush beneath the stone, and the sound of it was like a heartbeat.

"Good," Naava said. "Now sing."

"Sing what?"

"Anything. Sing the song of the morning. Sing the song of the millet. Sing the song of your own hands." Naava's voice was patient, unhurried. "The song is inside you, child. You only have to open your mouth and let it out."

Nankya opened her mouth. At first, nothing came. She felt foolish, kneeling there with the stone in her hands, her grandmother watching. But then, without meaning to, she began to hum. It was a small sound, barely audible, but it was there. It grew, and as it grew, it found words—not the words of Naava's songs, but words of her own, words she did not know she knew.

*The sun is rising, the sun is rising,*
*The millet is ground, the water is fetched,*
*The day is beginning, the day is beginning,*
*And I am here, and I am here…*

Naava's hands fell away. She sat back and watched her granddaughter sing. In the gray morning light, with the stone still moving, with the millet yielding beneath her hands, Nankya looked like every child who had ever sung the world into being. She looked like the beginning of something.

When Nankya stopped, breathless, Naava nodded. "That is a good song," she said. "It is a small song, but it is true. And truth is the seed of all songs." She stood and looked at the horizon, where the sun was beginning to rise. "Tomorrow," she said, "you will learn the first verse of the long water. But today, you have already begun."

Nankya looked at her hands, dusted with millet flour, and then at the sky. The sun was rising, and the world was singing, and she was part of it. She was only just beginning to understand what that meant.
