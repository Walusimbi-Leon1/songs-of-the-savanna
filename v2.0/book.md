### Chapter 2 — The Stone That Remembers

The grinding stone sang before Naava did. It was a low, patient groan—river rock against river rock, the sound older than any song, older than the village, older perhaps than the savanna itself. Nankya lay on her mat, one eye open in the gray light before sunrise, watching her grandmother's silhouette bend and straighten, bend and straighten, arms moving in a rhythm that had outlasted kingdoms.

The millet fell like pale rain. Naava's hands turned the top stone, and the whole compound breathed with it: the chickens shifting in their coop, the goat bleating once from its tether, the smoke from the cooking fire curling upward in a question.

Nankya waited. She knew better than to speak first. The songs came when they came, and they came when Naava decided.

The grinding slowed. Naava wiped her brow with the back of her wrist, looked at the sky, and began to hum. The hum was low, almost a growl, and it seemed to come from somewhere deep in the earth rather than from her throat. Nankya sat up slowly, her heart quickening.

*"When the sun was a child,"* Naava sang, her voice cracking into the morning air, *"it walked on two legs. When the sun was a child, it answered to the drum."*

Nankya knew this one. She had heard it a hundred times, but it never settled in her the same way twice. Today it felt different. Today, something in her grandmother's voice had shifted, an urgency underneath the melody like water moving beneath ice.

"Grandmother," Nankya said, the word slipping out before she could stop it.

Naava did not stop grinding. She did not turn. But the hum continued, and after a moment she said, "The song is not finished, child."

"I know. But—"

"Then listen."

Nankya closed her mouth. She pulled her cloth around her shoulders and sat cross-legged on her mat, watching the sun breach the distant acacia line. The grass was tall and gold, swaying in a wind she could not feel. The sky was the color of unripe mangoes. And her grandmother's voice folded into all of it, weaving itself into the fabric of the morning.

*"The sun walked with the first people. It walked with the first cows. It stood at the door of the first house and asked for water. And the first mother, she gave it milk. The first father, he gave it fire. The sun remembered. The sun remembers."*

Naava's hands stopped moving. The stone grew quiet. In the silence, Nankya heard the flies buzzing around the goat pen, heard her own breath, heard the far-off call of a hornbill.

"That is the song," Naava said, settling onto a low stool beside the grinding stone. She was not a tall woman, but sitting she seemed to take up more space than her body warranted. Her face was a map of lines—some deep, some shallow, all of them pointing toward eyes that missed nothing.

"But where was the sun going?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. It was a thin smile, barely a curve, but it warmed her whole face. "You ask the right question. Your mother never asked the right question. Neither did your father."

"Where was it going?"

"The sun was going home. But the sun had forgotten where home was. So the people sang it back. Every day, they sang the sun back across the sky. And that is why we sing, child. We are the ones who remember. We are the ones who sing things back to where they belong."

Nankya turned this over in her mind. It was like a stone in her palm, smooth and heavy, with hidden edges. "But the teacher says the sun is a star. A ball of fire in the sky. It doesn't have legs."

Naava laughed. It was a sharp, sudden sound, like a bird taking flight. "The teacher says many things. Some of them are true. Some of them are only true in the world the teacher lives in."

"Which world do we live in?"

Naava leaned forward. Her eyes, dark and steady, held Nankya's gaze. "We live in the world that was here before the teacher's world. We live in the world of the songs. And in that world, the sun walks, and the sun remembers, and the sun asks for water at the door of the first house. You will learn the teacher's world in the classroom. But you must learn our world here. Otherwise, you will be a person with only one eye, and one eye is not enough to see the whole sky."

Nankya felt a shiver run up her spine. It was not a cold shiver. It was the feeling of a door opening inside her, a door she had not known was there.

"Will you teach me?" she asked.

Naava stood. She walked to the cooking fire and stirred the pot of porridge that had been simmering. The steam rose, carrying the smell of maize and a hint of sweetness. "I am already teaching you," she said. "Every morning, you sit here. Every morning, you listen. That is how it is done. That is how it has always been done."

"But the songs—they're so many. How will I remember them all?"

"You will not remember them all." Naava's voice was matter-of-fact, without comfort. "No one remembers them all. The songs are not yours to remember. They are yours to carry for a while, and then to pass on."

"To who?"

Naava was quiet for a long moment. She ladled porridge into a clay bowl and handed it to Nankya. The bowl was warm in her hands, the porridge thick and smooth. "You will know," Naava said. "When the time comes, you will know. The song will tell you."

Nankya ate her porridge in silence, watching her grandmother move through the compound. Naava's movements were economical, precise—she wasted nothing, not in her body, not in her words. Everything she did had purpose. Everything she said had weight.

When the bowl was empty, Nankya set it down and asked the question she had been carrying all morning.

"Grandmother, what happened to the songs?"

Naava's back was to her. She was folding a cloth, her hands moving slowly, deliberately. "What do you mean?"

"The teacher said our songs are not real. He said they are stories that people made up because they did not know science. He said the real history is in the books."

Naava finished folding the cloth. She placed it on the ground, smoothed it once with her palm, and then turned to face Nankya. Her face was unreadable, but her voice was soft, softer than Nankya had ever heard it.

"The books," Naava said, "are written by people who were not there. The songs are sung by people who were. Which do you think is more real?"

"The songs?"

"The songs." Naava nodded. "But you must be careful, child. The books are strong. They are everywhere. They will tell you that you are small, that your people are small, that your history is small. They will tell you that the songs are lies. And if you are not careful, you will believe them. Because the books are loud, and the songs are quiet."

"Then how do I keep the songs loud in my head?"

Naava walked to Nankya and knelt in front of her. She took Nankya's hands in her own, and Nankya felt the roughness of her palms, the calluses from years of work. "You sing them," she said. "You sing them every day. You sing them when you are happy, and you sing them when you are sad. You sing them when you are alone, and you sing them when you are with others. You sing them until they are not in your head anymore, but in your bones. And then, when the books are loud, you will not hear them. You will only hear the songs."

Nankya looked at her grandmother's face. In the morning light, the lines seemed less deep, the eyes brighter. There was something ancient in Naava's gaze, something that had seen too much and yet kept seeing.

"Will you sing with me?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. This time, the smile was full. "Yes," she said. "I will sing with you. But first, you must learn to listen."

She stood and walked to the eastern edge of the compound, where the baobab tree stood sentinel. Its trunk was massive, gnarled, older than memory. Naava placed her palm against the bark and closed her eyes.

"Listen," she said.

Nankya walked to her grandmother's side and placed her own hand on the tree. The bark was rough, warm from the rising sun. She closed her eyes.

"What am I listening for?"

"The tree," Naava said. "The tree is singing. Can you hear it?"

Nankya listened. At first, she heard only the wind, the distant birds, the low rumble of a truck on the road far away. But then, beneath those sounds, she heard something else. A vibration. A hum. It was faint, almost imperceptible, but it was there, rising up from the roots through the trunk and into her palm.

"I hear it," she whispered.

Naava opened her eyes. "Good," she said. "Now you are ready."

The sun climbed higher. The compound filled with the sounds of the day—the clatter of pots, the laughter of children, the lowing of cattle being led to pasture. But Nankya stood at the baobab with her hand on the bark, listening. And in her chest, the song took root.

### Chapter 3 — The Song of the Long Water

The moon was still high when Naava's voice threaded through the compound, thin and certain, drawing Nankya from sleep. She lay on her mat for a moment, the song curling around her like smoke, and then she rose. No lamp, no lantern—the dark was a familiar garment, worn easily. She stepped over her sleeping brothers, careful where she placed her feet, and went out into the open air.

Naava was already at the grinding stone, her body bent over it in a silhouette the darkness could not fully claim. The song she sang was old—Nankya could tell by the shape of it, the way the words did not fit the mouth easily, the way they seemed to come from someplace deeper than breath. Naava's arms moved in a steady rhythm, the stone groaning against the millet, and the song moved with her, rising and falling like water finding its level.

*Kuno, kuno, amazzi gajja,*
*Kuno, kuno, gajja mu ngalo…*

Nankya sat on the packed earth near her grandmother's feet and listened. The words were familiar, but only in the way a dream is familiar—you know you have seen it before, but you cannot say where or when. She had heard this song many times, but this morning it seemed different. This morning, the words seemed to be reaching for something.

"Grandmother," she said, when the song paused for breath, "what is that song about?"

Naava did not stop grinding. "It is the song of the long water. The water that came from the sky and stayed. The water that gave us life when the land was dying."

"What long water?"

Naava's hands stilled. She looked at Nankya, and in the half-light of dawn, her eyes were two dark pools. "You have seen the lake? The one the road passes?"

Nankya nodded. She had been there once, with her father, when he had gone to sell goats at the trading post. The lake had stretched to the horizon, a sheet of silver under the sun, and she had stood at its edge and felt very small.

"That lake," Naava said, "was not always there. There was a time, long ago, when this land was dry. The rivers were dust. The animals walked until they fell. The people looked at the sky and saw only fire."

"What happened?"

Naava resumed her grinding. The stone sang its rough song. "There was a girl," she said. "A girl not much older than you. Her name was Nalwoga. She was the daughter of a chief, but she had no special power, no special gift. She was just a girl who loved her people. And when the land dried, and the people began to die, she could not bear it."

"What did she do?"

"She walked. She walked to the east, where the sun rises, because the elders said that is where the sky keeps its water. She walked for many days. Her feet cracked. Her throat closed. But she walked. And when she reached the edge of the world, where the sky meets the earth, she found a door."

"A door?"

"A door made of rain. It was locked, but it was not locked with iron. It was locked with silence." Naava's voice dropped, and the grinding slowed. "So Nalwoga sang. She sang the song of her people, the song of the land, the song of her mother and her mother's mother. She sang until her voice was raw, until her throat was blood, until the silence broke and the door opened."

"And then?"

"Then the water came. It came from the door, a great river of it, and it followed her home. It filled the dry riverbeds and the cracked earth. It filled the hollow of the land, where the lake is now. And the people drank, and the animals drank, and the land lived again."

Nankya sat very still. The story sat in her chest like a warm stone. "Is that true?"

Naava's hands stopped. She looked at Nankya for a long moment. "Is what true? That a girl walked to the edge of the world? That she sang a song that opened a door made of rain?"

"Yes. All of it."

Naava wiped her hands on her apron. "The lake is there," she said. "The water is there. The song is there. That is what I know. The rest—" She shrugged. "The teacher would say it is a story. But the lake does not care what the teacher says. The lake is full. The lake is there."

Nankya turned the words over in her mind. She had seen the lake. She had stood at its edge and felt the damp wind on her face. She had seen the fishermen on its surface, their canoes like water bugs. The lake was real. The lake was enormous. And now her grandmother was telling her that it had been sung into being by a girl named Nalwoga.

"Was Nalwoga real?" she asked.

Naava tilted her head. "Was she real? Your mother is real. Your father is real. Your brothers are real. But where are they now?"

"At home. In the village."

"And where is the village?"

"Here."

"And where is here?"

Nankya frowned. "I don't understand."

Naava stood and walked to the cooking fire. She stirred the embers, blew on them, coaxed a small flame to life. "The teacher would say that the village is at a certain latitude and longitude. He would say it is on a map, between this road and that road. He would say it has a certain number of people and a certain number of houses. All of that is true. But is that what makes the village real?"

Nankya shook her head slowly.

"No," Naava said. "The village is real because your mother was born here. Because your grandfather is buried here. Because the first tree was planted here by a woman whose name we no longer remember, but whose hands we still use. The village is real because the songs are sung here. And the songs are real because they are sung. That is what makes something real, child. Not whether it happened. Whether it is remembered."

"But the teacher said—"

"The teacher is not here." Naava's voice was firm, but not unkind. "The teacher is in his classroom, teaching his books. But you are here. And I am here. And the sun is rising, and the millet is ground, and we are alive. That is the truth I know."

Nankya was quiet for a long moment. The fire crackled. The first birds began to call, their voices threading through the gray light. She thought about Nalwoga walking to the edge of the world. She thought about the door made of rain. She thought about the lake, vast and silver, and how she had stood at its edge and felt it pulling at her, as if it recognized her.

"Grandmother," she said, "will you teach me the song of the long water?"

Naava smiled. It was a slow smile, spreading across her face like water finding its level. "That is a long song," she said. "It takes many days to learn. And it is not a song you can rush. It is a song you must grow into."

"I want to grow into it."

Naava looked at her granddaughter, and in her eyes, Nankya saw something shift. A door opening. A recognition. "You have your grandmother's stubbornness," Naava said. "And your mother's eyes. And your father's heart." She reached out and touched Nankya's cheek with her rough palm. "The song will come. But first, we must build your bones. A song like that cannot live in a weak body. It needs a strong house."

"What do I have to do?"

"You will do what I do. Every morning, you will grind the millet. You will fetch the water. You will tend the fire. You will learn the work of the body, so that the body can carry the work of the song." Naava's voice softened. "The songs do not live in the voice alone, child. They live in the hands, and the feet, and the back. They live in the way you stand and the way you walk. A song is not only sung. It is lived."

Nankya stood. She walked to the grinding stone and placed her hands on the smooth, worn surface. It was warm from Naava's work, and she could feel the ghost of the motion, the rhythm her grandmother had pressed into it over the years. She picked up the smaller stone and began to grind, her movements clumsy at first, the millet slipping and scattering.

"Like this," Naava said, and she knelt beside Nankya, placing her hands over Nankya's, guiding the motion. "Slowly. The stone is not in a hurry. The millet is not in a hurry. You are not in a hurry."

Nankya let her grandmother's hands move hers. The rhythm was strange, but she could feel it taking shape, the circle of it, the patience of it. The millet began to crush beneath the stone, and the sound of it was like a heartbeat.

"Good," Naava said. "Now sing."

"Sing what?"

"Anything. Sing the song of the morning. Sing the song of the millet. Sing the song of your own hands." Naava's voice was patient, unhurried. "The song is inside you, child. You only have to open your mouth and let it out."

Nankya opened her mouth. At first, nothing came. She felt foolish, kneeling there with the stone in her hands, her grandmother watching. But then, without meaning to, she began to hum. It was a small sound, barely audible, but it was there. It grew, and as it grew, it found words—not the words of Naava's songs, but words of her own, words she did not know she knew.

*The sun is rising, the sun is rising,*
*The millet is ground, the water is fetched,*
*The day is beginning, the day is beginning,*
*And I am here, and I am here…*

Naava's hands fell away. She sat back and watched her granddaughter sing. In the gray morning light, with the stone still moving, with the millet yielding beneath her hands, Nankya looked like every child who had ever sung the world into being. She looked like the beginning of something.

When Nankya stopped, breathless, Naava nodded. "That is a good song," she said. "It is a small song, but it is true. And truth is the seed of all songs." She stood and looked at the horizon, where the sun was beginning to rise. "Tomorrow," she said, "you will learn the first verse of the long water. But today, you have already begun."

Nankya looked at her hands, dusted with millet flour, and then at the sky. The sun was rising, and the world was singing, and she was part of it. She was only just beginning to understand what that meant.

### Chapter 4 — The Song of the Long Water

The next morning came the way mornings come in the dry season: not slowly, but all at once, as if the sun had been waiting impatiently behind the horizon and finally decided to push through. Nankya was already awake when the first light touched the thatch of the roof. She had slept badly, dreaming of water — great sheets of it, silver and unbroken, stretching to the edge of everything.

She found Naava at the grinding stone, as she always was, her body moving in the old rhythm. But this morning, there was something different in the air. A stillness. A waiting.

"Sit," Naava said, without turning around.

Nankya sat. The ground was cool beneath her, the dew still heavy on the grass. She watched her grandmother's back, the way it bent and straightened, the way her arms moved in their patient circle. She had seen this motion a thousand times, had imitated it as a small child with a pebble and a flat stone, pretending to grind, pretending to be Naava. But today she saw it differently. Today she saw it as the beginning of something.

Naava finished her work. She scooped the flour into a clay bowl, set it aside, and wiped her hands on her apron. Then she turned and looked at Nankya with an expression the girl had never seen before — not stern, not soft, but something in between. Weighing. Measuring.

"The song of the long water," Naava said, "is not one song. It is many songs, woven together like the reeds of a basket. Each verse is a strand. Each verse is a day. Each verse is a woman who walked before you and left her footprints in the song."

She sat down across from Nankya, her legs folded beneath her, her hands resting on her knees. The sun was fully up now, and the light fell across her face, illuminating the deep lines around her eyes and mouth. She looked ancient. She looked like the beginning of the world.

"In the beginning," Naava said, "there was no lake. There was only the dry ground, and the dry grass, and the dry sky. The rivers had forgotten their way. The rains had forgotten to come. The animals had forgotten where the water was, and so they wandered, and so they died."

Nankya leaned forward. She had heard stories of the dry times before, but never like this. Never with this weight in her grandmother's voice.

"The people gathered at the edge of the savanna," Naava continued. "They were many, but they were weak. Their children were thirsty. Their old ones were dying. They looked at the sky, and the sky was empty. They looked at the ground, and the ground was cracked. They had no songs for this. Their songs had been about rain, about rivers, about plenty. But those songs had dried up too."

She paused, and in the pause, Nankya could hear the birds, the insects, the small sounds of the world going about its business.

"One girl did not sit with the others. She walked. She walked away from the people, away from the cracking ground, away from the dying animals. She walked until she was alone, and then she walked some more. Her name was Nalwoga."

"Nalwoga," Nankya repeated. The name felt strange in her mouth, heavy as a stone.

"Nalwoga," Naava confirmed. "And as she walked, she did not sing. She had no song. She had only thirst, and grief, and a small, stubborn hope that if she walked far enough, she would find something. She walked for three days. On the third day, her legs gave out, and she fell."

Naava's voice dropped, almost to a whisper.

"She lay on the ground and looked up at the sky. And the sky spoke to her. Not in words, but in a feeling. A feeling like a hand pressing down on her chest, saying, *Get up. You are not finished.* And she got up. And she began to walk again. But this time, she did not walk in silence. She opened her mouth, and a sound came out. Not a word. Not a melody. Just a sound. A vibration. A hum."

Nankya felt the hair on her arms stand up. She knew that hum. She had felt it in her own chest the morning before, rising unbidden, finding its way out of her.

"The hum grew," Naava said. "It found shape. It found rhythm. It found words. And the words were not words she had been taught. They were words that had been waiting in the dry ground, in the dry air, in the dry bones of her ancestors. They were words that had been there all along, sleeping."

Naava began to sing. Her voice was low, rough as bark, but it carried a melody that seemed older than the earth itself. The words were in a language Nankya did not fully understand — the old language, the one the teachers called dead, the one the schoolbooks said had never existed — and yet she understood. She understood with her chest, with her belly, with the roots of her hair.

*Nalwoga, Nalwoga, child of the dry ground,*
*Open your mouth and let the water sound.*
*The rivers are sleeping, the rains are far,*
*But the song in your blood knows where they are.*

Naava stopped. The silence that followed was deeper than silence. It was the silence of the world listening.

"She sang that verse, and the ground trembled. She sang it again, and the ground cracked. She sang it a third time, and from the crack came water. Not a trickle. Not a stream. A flood. A great, surging wall of water, rising from the earth, spreading across the savanna, filling the low places, carving new paths, making the lake where no lake had been."

Nankya's breath caught. "The lake was made from a song?"

"The lake was made from a song," Naava said. "But not just the song. The song was the key. The water was already there, sleeping beneath the ground, waiting. All living things are made of water, child. The ground remembers. The ground keeps what it is given. Nalwoga's song was not creating something new. It was calling something home."

The fire crackled between them. A chicken scratched in the dirt. The world continued, ordinary and unchanged, and yet nothing would ever be ordinary again.

"How do you know?" Nankya asked. Her voice was small. "How do you know it was her? How do you know it happened?"

Naava looked at her for a long moment. Then she stood, and walked to the edge of the compound, and pointed at the horizon. In the distance, shimmering with heat, was the lake. It was a long walk from the village, farther than Nankya had ever gone alone, but she had been there. She had seen it.

"Walk there," Naava said. "Put your hand in the water. Taste it. Feel it move against your skin. Then ask me again how I know."

Nankya stood and joined her grandmother at the edge of the compound. The lake was a silver line in the distance, barely visible through the heat haze. It seemed impossibly far. It seemed impossibly real.

"When will you teach me the song?" she asked.

"I will teach you today," Naava said. "Not the whole song — that would take a lifetime, and you are only twelve. But I will teach you the first verse. The verse that woke the water. And you will learn it, and you will carry it, and when you are ready, you will sing it."

"What if I'm not ready?"

Naava turned to look at her. Her eyes were bright, sharp, alive.

"You were born ready," she said. "You just don't know it yet."

She knelt in the dirt and smoothed a patch of ground with her palm. Then she picked up a stick and began to draw in the earth. Nankya watched as lines appeared — not letters, not numbers, but shapes. Spirals. Curves. A pattern that seemed to move even as it lay still.

"This is the shape of the song," Naava said. "Each curve is a breath. Each spiral is a word. The song is not just sound, child. It is shape. It is movement. It is the way the water moves, and the way the wind moves, and the way your own blood moves in your veins. To sing the song of the long water, you must become the water."

Nankya knelt beside her and studied the pattern. It was simple, and it was not simple. It was a spiral within a spiral, a curve within a curve, and at the center of it all, a single point.

"What is that?" she asked, pointing.

"That," Naava said, "is you. The song begins with you. It always begins with the one who sings it. Nalwoga did not sing to the water. She sang to herself, and the water heard her, and it came."

The sun was higher now, and the heat was beginning to press down on them. The village was waking around them — doors creaking, voices calling, the low murmur of the day beginning. But Nankya and Naava were in their own world, a world drawn in the dirt, a world of spirals and songs and water that remembered.

"Close your eyes," Naava said.

Nankya closed her eyes.

"Breathe. Feel the air in your lungs. Feel the ground beneath your knees. Feel the heat of the sun on your skin. Feel everything, because everything is part of the song."

Nankya breathed. The air was warm and smelled of dust and cooking fires. The ground was hard beneath her knees. The sun pressed against her closed eyelids, red and bright. She felt it all, and as she felt it, something began to stir in her chest. A hum. A vibration. The same hum she had felt the morning before, but stronger now. More insistent.

"Now open your mouth," Naava said. "And sing."

### Chapter 5 — The Taste of the Lake

The first verse came out of Nankya's mouth like a stone dropped into deep water. It was not a song. It was not even a sound she recognized as her own. It was a rough, scraping noise, a cough, a swallowed thing that had been sitting in her throat for twelve years without her knowing it.

Naava did not laugh. She did not correct her. She simply waited.

"Again," she said.

Nankya opened her mouth and tried again. The sound was different this time — lower, stranger, like something stirring in a cave. She felt it in her chest first, a vibration that traveled up through her throat and out into the air, and for a moment she thought she heard something in it. A shape. A movement. The beginning of a curve.

"Again," Naava said.

She sang the line the way Naava had sung it, letting the unfamiliar syllables roll off her tongue, stumbling over the hard consonants, swallowing the soft ones. She sang it a fourth time, a fifth, a sixth, until the words stopped being foreign and started being hers. By the tenth repetition, she was no longer thinking about the words at all. She was thinking about the shape of them. The way they curved and spiraled and moved.

The sun climbed higher. The village sounds grew louder around them. But Nankya kept singing, and Naava kept listening.

——

She learned the second verse that afternoon, after the porridge was eaten and the goats were watered. She learned it while she walked to the well, the words circling in her head like birds. She learned it while she fetched water, the clay pot heavy against her hip, her lips moving silently as she counted the syllables, felt the rhythm of them against her teeth.

By evening, she knew both verses. Not perfectly. Not the way Naava knew them, the way Naava *was* them. But she knew them. They were in her now, planted like seeds in dry ground, waiting for rain.

"The song is growing," Naava said, as they sat outside her grandmother's hut, watching the sun bleed orange and red across the sky. "I can hear it in you. It is finding its home."

Nankya touched her throat. It felt different somehow. Fuller. As if something had taken root there and was putting down roots, reaching deep into her chest, into her belly, into the place where her breath began.

"Can I sing it at the lake?" she asked.

Naava was silent for a long moment. The fire crackled. A distant dog barked. Somewhere far away, a mother called her child in from the dark.

"Not yet," Naava said finally. "The song is not finished with you. You have the first two verses, but the song has more. And the lake will not hear you until you can sing it whole."

"How many verses are there?"

"Seven."

Nankya felt the number settle over her like a weight. Seven. She had two. Five more stretching ahead of her like a road she could not see the end of.

"How long will it take?"

Naava smiled, a slow, deep smile that creased her face like riverbeds. "As long as it takes. You cannot rush a song, child. You cannot hurry water. You can only wait, and listen, and let it come to you."

———

The third verse came to her in the night.

Nankya woke from a dream she could not remember, the way you wake from a fall — suddenly, breathless, heart pounding. The air was cool and still. Through the gap in the wall, she could see a sliver of moon, thin as a fingernail, hanging over the dark line of the horizon.

And then she heard it.

Not with her ears. With her chest. A hum, low and insistent, rising up from somewhere deep inside her, the same hum she had felt that first morning, the same hum that had risen in her throat when she had tried to sing. But now it had words. Words she had never learned. Words she had never heard. Words that came to her as naturally as breath.

She opened her mouth and let them out.

The sound was small in the darkness, barely a whisper, but it filled the hut. It filled her. She sang the verse three times, four times, letting the syllables shape themselves, feeling the curve of each one against her tongue. And then, as suddenly as it had come, it was gone. The hum settled back into silence. The words faded. But they were still there. She could feel them, waiting, nested in her like birds in a tree.

The next morning, she sang it for Naava. Her grandmother listened, her eyes closed, her face still as stone. When Nankya finished, Naava opened her eyes and nodded slowly.

"The song is finding you," she said. "You did not learn it. It came to you. That is how it works. The song chooses its singer. You cannot force it. You can only be ready."

———

The fourth verse came three days later, while Nankya was walking home from the well. She was thinking about nothing in particular — thinking about the weight of the pot, the heat of the sun, the way the dust felt between her toes — when the words simply arrived, sliding into her mind like a key into a lock. She stopped walking. The pot swayed on her head. She opened her mouth and sang.

The words were strange and old, full of sounds she had never made before, and yet they fit her mouth perfectly, as if they had been waiting there all along. She sang the verse twice, three times, until she was sure of it, and then she continued walking, her heart full and light, the song settled inside her like a stone in a riverbed.

The fifth verse came to her in her sleep, in a dream of rain. She woke with the words on her lips and the feeling of water on her skin.

———

"Five," Nankya said, sitting cross-legged in the shade of the acacia tree, watching Naava pound millet in a wooden mortar. "I have five."

Naava did not stop pounding. The rhythmic thump of the pestle filled the air, steady as a heartbeat. "Five," she repeated. "That is good. That is more than many will ever learn. But the song is not complete. You have not yet sung the verse that wakes the water."

"How do I find it?"

Naava set down the pestle and looked at her granddaughter. Her face was unreadable, carved from the same dark wood as the mortar, but her eyes were soft.

"You do not find it," she said. "It finds you. And it will find you when you are ready to hear it. Not before. There is no rushing the water, child. There is no hurrying the rain."

———

The sixth verse found her on the morning of the ninth day.

Nankya was sitting at the edge of the compound, watching the sun rise over the distant line of the lake. The heat of the day had not yet begun; the air was cool and clean, and the world felt new, as if it had just been made. She was not thinking about the song. She was thinking about the lake. About what it would feel like to stand at its edge, to put her hand in the water, to taste it. To see, with her own eyes, the thing her grandmother's song had made.

And then the words were there.

They came without warning, without effort, rising up from the place inside her where the other verses lived. She opened her mouth and they flowed out, smooth as water, perfect as rain. She sang the verse once, and then again, and then she stood up, her heart pounding, her hands trembling.

"Naava!" she called. "Naava, come!"

Her grandmother came out of the hut, wiping her hands on her apron. She walked across the compound, her steps slow and deliberate, and when she reached Nankya she looked at her for a long moment without speaking.

"The sixth verse has come to you," Naava said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Nankya said. "It just came. I didn't even know I was looking for it."

Naava nodded slowly. "That is when it comes. When you stop looking. When you are not trying." She reached out and placed her hand on Nankya's chest, over her heart. "It is there now. All of it except the last. Are you ready?"

Nankya felt the weight of the question. Six verses. Six days of the song growing in her, putting down roots, becoming part of her. And now, only one remained. The verse that woke the water. The verse that had made the lake.

"Will you take me?" she asked. "Will you take me to the lake?"

Naava looked at her for a long time. The sun was higher now, the heat beginning to press down. The village was stirring around them, the sounds of the day beginning.

"Tomorrow," Naava said. "Tomorrow, at dawn. We will walk to the lake. And we will see what the song has made."

———

That night, Nankya could not sleep.

She lay on her mat, staring at the darkness of the ceiling, feeling the six verses moving inside her like a current. She sang them silently, one after another, letting the words flow through her mind. The first verse. The second. The third that had come in the night. The fourth that had come walking home from the well. The fifth in the dream of rain. The sixth that morning, at the edge of the compound.

Six verses. One more.

She closed her eyes and tried to feel the seventh waiting somewhere inside her, but there was only silence. Emptiness. The place where the song should be was still dark, still waiting, still unformed.

But it was there. She could feel it, the way you can feel a storm coming even before you see the clouds. It was there, gathering, waiting, ready to break.

She fell asleep with her hand over her heart, feeling the hum of the song beneath her skin, and she dreamed of water.

### Chapter 6 — The Verse That Wakes the Water

The dream was all water. Not the angry water of storms, but a vast, still water, flat as a mirror, reflecting a sky so wide it had no edges. Nankya stood on the shore and the water stretched out before her, dark and deep and patient, and she knew — the way you know things in dreams — that it was waiting for her. That it had always been waiting for her. That it would wait forever if it had to.

She woke to the sound of Naava's voice. Not singing. Calling.

"Nankya. Wake. The sun is not yet up, but it is thinking about it."

Nankya opened her eyes. The hut was grey with the first light, that thin, uncertain light that comes before dawn, when the world has not yet decided what it wants to be. She sat up. Her body was stiff from sleep, but her mind was sharp, alert, the way it got when something important was about to happen. She could feel the six verses inside her, humming their low, steady hum. And beyond them, beyond the six, the silence where the seventh should be. The silence that waited.

She dressed quickly — a simple dress, cotton, faded blue, and her good sandals that she saved for church. Naava was already outside, standing by the cooking fire with a calabash of water and a bundle wrapped in banana leaves. She had dressed herself in her good cloth, the one with the indigo border that she wore only for ceremonies. Her grey hair was wrapped in a headscarf the color of earth.

"You are ready," Naava said. It was not a question.

Nankya nodded. She took the calabash and drank. The water was cool and tasted of clay. She ate a piece of cassava from the bundle, chewed slowly, and then they set out.

The path to the lake began at the edge of the village, where the last compound ended and the grass began. It was not a real path — not the kind with packed earth and clear borders — but more of a suggestion, a memory of feet that had walked this way before. The grass was tall, silver with dew, and it parted before them like a curtain. The sky above was still dark in the west, but the east was bleeding orange, a thin line of fire along the horizon that was spreading upward, turning the grey to gold.

They walked in silence. Naava led, her steps slow but sure, her back straight, her face turned toward the horizon. Nankya followed, matching her grandmother's pace, feeling the cool air on her skin and the soft earth beneath her sandals. The savanna opened around them — wide and flat and endless, the grass rippling in a wind she could barely feel. Here and there, a tree broke the skyline, an acacia or a mango, its branches black against the brightening sky. A bird called somewhere, high and sweet, and another answered, and then another, until the air was threaded with birdsong.

They walked for a long time. The sun rose. The dew burned off the grass. The heat began to gather, slow and patient, the way it always did. Naava did not speak, and Nankya did not ask her to. There was something about the walking itself that felt like singing — the rhythm of their steps, the rise and fall of the land, the slow wheeling of the sky above them. She felt the verses inside her, moving with her body, swaying like grass in the wind.

After a while, the land began to change. The grass grew shorter. The earth turned darker, richer, the color of wet clay. The air changed too — it grew heavier, thicker, carrying a smell that Nankya had no name for, a smell that was old and deep and alive. The smell of water.

"Grandmother," she said.

"I smell it," Naava said. "We are close."

They crested a low rise, and there it was.

The lake.

It was not what Nankya had expected. She had imagined something small, something she could see across, something with clear edges and a beginning and an end. But this — this was vast. It spread out before them, silver and grey, its surface so still it looked solid, like a sheet of beaten metal. The far shore was invisible, swallowed by distance and haze. The sky reflected in it perfectly, so that it was impossible to tell where the water ended and the air began. It was as if the world had been folded in half, and they were standing at the crease.

Nankya stopped. Her breath caught in her chest. The six verses inside her went suddenly, completely silent, as if they, too, were holding their breath.

"Walk," Naava said gently. "Come. Walk with me."

They descended the rise. The ground was soft now, spongy underfoot, and small pools of water gathered in the depressions, reflecting the sky in miniature. Reeds grew along the edge, tall and green, swaying in a breeze that came off the water. A heron stood in the shallows, motionless, watching them with one bright eye. The smell of water was everywhere — in the air, in the earth, in the grass. Nankya felt it on her skin, a coolness that had nothing to do with temperature.

They reached the shore. The water lay before them, flat and perfect and ancient. Naava set down her bundle and stood, her hands at her sides, her face turned toward the lake. She stood for a long time, and Nankya stood with her, and neither of them spoke.

Then Naava turned to her and said, "Sit."

Nankya sat. The earth was cool and damp beneath her. Naava sat beside her, their shoulders almost touching, and together they looked out over the water.

"This lake," Naava said, her voice low and steady, "was not always here. When your grandfather's grandfather was a boy, this was dry land. Grass and dust and nothing more. The people drank from a well — a deep well, the deepest in the land — and when the well ran dry, they walked three days to the river. Three days each way. Many died on the walk. The children. The old ones. They died of thirst in a land that had forgotten rain."

Nankya listened. The words settled over her like a blanket.

"There was a woman," Naava continued. "She lived at the edge of the village. She was not married. She had no children. She was old, older than anyone could remember, and people said she was mad because she talked to the sky. She would stand at the edge of the compound every evening and sing to the sky, sing to the clouds, sing to the rain that would not come. People laughed at her. They threw stones at her. They told her to be silent."

Naava paused. Her eyes were fixed on the water, but Nankya could see that she was seeing something else — something that was not there anymore, something that existed only in memory and song.

"But she did not stop singing. She sang every night for three years. She sang the songs of her mother, and her mother's mother, and all the mothers back to the beginning. She sang every verse she knew. And when she had sung them all, she sang new ones. Verses that came to her in dreams. Verses that came to her in the middle of the day, when she was cooking or fetching water or sitting in the shade. Verses that came from somewhere deep inside her, from a place she did not know she had."

"The place where my verses come from," Nankya whispered.

Naava looked at her. "Yes," she said. "That place. That is the place. The old people called it the deep. The place where the songs live. The place where the memory of the world is kept. It is in every person, but most people forget it. Most people fill it up with other things — with noise, with hurry, with the things the schoolbooks teach. But the songs are still there. Waiting."

"What happened to the woman?" Nankya asked.

"She sang for three years," Naava said. "And on the last night of the third year, she sang a song that had never been sung before. A song of seven verses. The first six verses she knew — they had been passed down to her, mother to daughter, for longer than anyone could remember. But the seventh verse — the verse that wakes the water — that verse had never been sung. It had never existed. It was waiting to be made. And she made it. She stood at the edge of the compound, and she opened her mouth, and the seventh verse came out of her, and when she finished singing, the ground shook."

Nankya's hands were trembling. She pressed them flat against the earth.

"The ground shook," Naava repeated. "And water came up. From the dry earth, from the dust, water came up. It came up slowly at first — a trickle, a seep, a darkening of the soil. And then faster. A spring. A stream. A flood. The water spread across the dry land and it did not stop. It filled the low places and the hollows and the dry riverbeds, and by morning, where there had been dust, there was this." She swept her hand toward the lake. "This."

They sat in silence. A fish broke the surface of the water — a quick silver flash, gone in an instant — and the ripples spread outward in perfect circles, growing wider and wider until they vanished.

"The schoolbooks," Nankya said. "They don't say anything about this."

"No," Naava said. "They do not. The schoolbooks say the lake was always here. They say it is a natural feature of the landscape, formed by geological processes over thousands of years. They say it in English, in big words that are meant to sound true. But they do not know this lake. They have never stood at its edge and felt the hum of the song beneath the water. They have never heard the seventh verse."

"Why not?" Nankya asked. "Why don't they know?"

Naava was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, measured, the voice she used when the truth was sharp.

"Because knowing requires listening," she said. "And they have forgotten how to listen. They listen only to themselves. To their own stories. To their own songs. The songs of other people — the songs of this land, of this water, of the people who were here before them — those songs they call myths. Legends. Folklore. They write them down in books, but they do not hear them. They study them the way you study a dead thing, turning them over, pinning them down, taking them apart. But a song is not a dead thing. A song is alive. It changes. It grows. It finds its singer."

She looked at Nankya, and her eyes were bright with something that might have been tears, or might have been the reflection of the water.

"That is why you are here," she said. "That is why the songs chose you. Not because you are special. Not because you are better than anyone else. But because you listened. Because you were quiet enough, and still enough, and open enough to hear them. The songs need a voice, child. They need someone to carry them. Someone who will not forget."

Nankya looked out at the lake. The sun was higher now, the water gleaming, the heat pressing down. She could feel the six verses inside her, steady and sure. And beyond them, the silence. The empty place where the seventh verse should be.

"Grandmother," she said. "I don't have it. The seventh verse. It hasn't come to me."

"I know," Naava said. "It will not come here. It will not come because you want it to. It will come when it is ready."

"But you brought me here. You said we would see what the song has made."

"We have seen it," Naava said. She gestured at the lake, at the water, at the vast silver expanse that stretched to the horizon. "We are seeing it now. The seventh verse made this. The seventh verse called this water up from the earth, and it is still calling it. The song does not end. It is still being sung."

Nankya looked at her grandmother. "Who is singing it now?"

Naava smiled. It was a small smile, barely a curve of the lips, but it transformed her face, made it young, made it luminous.

"The lake is singing it," she said. "The water is the song. Can you not hear it?"

Nankya listened. She listened with her ears, and she heard nothing but the soft lapping of water against the shore, the distant cry of a bird, the whisper of wind through the reeds. But then she listened with the other part of her — the deep place, the place where the songs lived — and she heard it. Faintly, far away, but growing closer. A hum. Low and steady and ancient. The hum of water. The hum of a song that had never stopped singing.

She closed her eyes. The hum grew louder. It filled her chest, her throat, her head. It was the same hum she had felt that first morning, the same hum that had risen in her when she had tried to sing. But now it was stronger, deeper, richer. It carried the weight of all six verses and something else — something new, something that was not yet words but was becoming words, forming itself in the dark, rising like water from the earth.

She opened her mouth. She did not decide to open it. It opened on its own, the way a flower opens, and the seventh verse came out.

It was not a verse at all. Not in the way the other six were verses — discrete, separate, each with its own shape and sound. This was something larger, something that held all the other verses inside it the way the lake held the sky. It was a sound that was also a feeling, a feeling that was also a memory, a memory that was also a promise. It poured out of her, and she could not stop it, and she did not want to stop it, and it went on and on, filling the air above the lake, spreading across the water like the ripples from a stone, reaching the far shore and the sky beyond and the place where the sky ends and something else begins.

When it was finished, she was crying. She did not know when she had started. The tears ran down her face and fell into the earth, and the earth drank them, and the lake glittered in the sun, and Naava sat beside her, silent, still, her face turned toward the water.

They sat for a long time. The sun climbed. The heat pressed down. The lake shimmered and breathed.

Then Naava reached over and took Nankya's hand. Her grip was strong, her fingers rough and warm.

"Now you have it," she said. "All seven. Now you are the singer."

Nankya looked at her grandmother. "What do I do with it?"

Naava squeezed her hand. "You live with it," she said. "You carry it. You sing it when the time is right, and you keep it silent when the time is not. You pass it on, when the time comes, to someone who will listen. That is all. That is everything."

Nankya looked out at the lake. The water was still. The sky was wide. Somewhere in the distance, a fish jumped, and the sound of it — the small, bright splash — carried across the water like a note in a song.

She carried seven verses now. Seven verses that the schoolbooks did not contain. Seven verses that no teacher would ever write on a blackboard. Seven verses that held the history of a people, a land, a lake that had been sung into being. They were hers, but they were not only hers. They belonged to the woman who had first sung them, and to all the women who had carried them before, and to the land itself, and to the water. They belonged to the future, too — to the person who would one day sit where Nankya was sitting and listen, and be quiet enough, and still enough, to hear.

The lake hummed. The sun blazed. The savanna stretched out around them, vast and golden and alive.

Nankya closed her eyes and held her grandmother's hand, and she listened.
