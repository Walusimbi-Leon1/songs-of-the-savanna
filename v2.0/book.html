Chapter 2 — The Stone That Remembers

The grinding stone sang before Naava did. It was a low, patient groan—river rock against river rock, the sound older than any song, older than the village, older perhaps than the savanna itself. Nankya lay on her mat, one eye open in the gray light before sunrise, watching her grandmother's silhouette bend and straighten, bend and straighten, arms moving in a rhythm that had outlasted kingdoms.

The millet fell like pale rain. Naava's hands turned the top stone, and the whole compound breathed with it: the chickens shifting in their coop, the goat bleating once from its tether, the smoke from the cooking fire curling upward in a question.

Nankya waited. She knew better than to speak first. The songs came when they came, and they came when Naava decided.

The grinding slowed. Naava wiped her brow with the back of her wrist, looked at the sky, and began to hum. The hum was low, almost a growl, and it seemed to come from somewhere deep in the earth rather than from her throat. Nankya sat up slowly, her heart quickening.

*"When the sun was a child,"* Naava sang, her voice cracking into the morning air, *"it walked on two legs. When the sun was a child, it answered to the drum."*

Nankya knew this one. She had heard it a hundred times, but it never settled in her the same way twice. Today it felt different. Today, something in her grandmother's voice had shifted, an urgency underneath the melody like water moving beneath ice.

"Grandmother," Nankya said, the word slipping out before she could stop it.

Naava did not stop grinding. She did not turn. But the hum continued, and after a moment she said, "The song is not finished, child."

"I know. But—"

"Then listen."

Nankya closed her mouth. She pulled her cloth around her shoulders and sat cross-legged on her mat, watching the sun breach the distant acacia line. The grass was tall and gold, swaying in a wind she could not feel. The sky was the color of unripe mangoes. And her grandmother's voice folded into all of it, weaving itself into the fabric of the morning.

*"The sun walked with the first people. It walked with the first cows. It stood at the door of the first house and asked for water. And the first mother, she gave it milk. The first father, he gave it fire. The sun remembered. The sun remembers."*

Naava's hands stopped moving. The stone grew quiet. In the silence, Nankya heard the flies buzzing around the goat pen, heard her own breath, heard the far-off call of a hornbill.

"That is the song," Naava said, settling onto a low stool beside the grinding stone. She was not a tall woman, but sitting she seemed to take up more space than her body warranted. Her face was a map of lines—some deep, some shallow, all of them pointing toward eyes that missed nothing.

"But where was the sun going?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. It was a thin smile, barely a curve, but it warmed her whole face. "You ask the right question. Your mother never asked the right question. Neither did your father."

"Where was it going?"

"The sun was going home. But the sun had forgotten where home was. So the people sang it back. Every day, they sang the sun back across the sky. And that is why we sing, child. We are the ones who remember. We are the ones who sing things back to where they belong."

Nankya turned this over in her mind. It was like a stone in her palm, smooth and heavy, with hidden edges. "But the teacher says the sun is a star. A ball of fire in the sky. It doesn't have legs."

Naava laughed. It was a sharp, sudden sound, like a bird taking flight. "The teacher says many things. Some of them are true. Some of them are only true in the world the teacher lives in."

"Which world do we live in?"

Naava leaned forward. Her eyes, dark and steady, held Nankya's gaze. "We live in the world that was here before the teacher's world. We live in the world of the songs. And in that world, the sun walks, and the sun remembers, and the sun asks for water at the door of the first house. You will learn the teacher's world in the classroom. But you must learn our world here. Otherwise, you will be a person with only one eye, and one eye is not enough to see the whole sky."

Nankya felt a shiver run up her spine. It was not a cold shiver. It was the feeling of a door opening inside her, a door she had not known was there.

"Will you teach me?" she asked.

Naava stood. She walked to the cooking fire and stirred the pot of porridge that had been simmering. The steam rose, carrying the smell of maize and a hint of sweetness. "I am already teaching you," she said. "Every morning, you sit here. Every morning, you listen. That is how it is done. That is how it has always been done."

"But the songs—they're so many. How will I remember them all?"

"You will not remember them all." Naava's voice was matter-of-fact, without comfort. "No one remembers them all. The songs are not yours to remember. They are yours to carry for a while, and then to pass on."

"To who?"

Naava was quiet for a long moment. She ladled porridge into a clay bowl and handed it to Nankya. The bowl was warm in her hands, the porridge thick and smooth. "You will know," Naava said. "When the time comes, you will know. The song will tell you."

Nankya ate her porridge in silence, watching her grandmother move through the compound. Naava's movements were economical, precise—she wasted nothing, not in her body, not in her words. Everything she did had purpose. Everything she said had weight.

When the bowl was empty, Nankya set it down and asked the question she had been carrying all morning.

"Grandmother, what happened to the songs?"

Naava's back was to her. She was folding a cloth, her hands moving slowly, deliberately. "What do you mean?"

"The teacher said our songs are not real. He said they are stories that people made up because they did not know science. He said the real history is in the books."

Naava finished folding the cloth. She placed it on the ground, smoothed it once with her palm, and then turned to face Nankya. Her face was unreadable, but her voice was soft, softer than Nankya had ever heard it.

"The books," Naava said, "are written by people who were not there. The songs are sung by people who were. Which do you think is more real?"

"The songs?"

"The songs." Naava nodded. "But you must be careful, child. The books are strong. They are everywhere. They will tell you that you are small, that your people are small, that your history is small. They will tell you that the songs are lies. And if you are not careful, you will believe them. Because the books are loud, and the songs are quiet."

"Then how do I keep the songs loud in my head?"

Naava walked to Nankya and knelt in front of her. She took Nankya's hands in her own, and Nankya felt the roughness of her palms, the calluses from years of work. "You sing them," she said. "You sing them every day. You sing them when you are happy, and you sing them when you are sad. You sing them when you are alone, and you sing them when you are with others. You sing them until they are not in your head anymore, but in your bones. And then, when the books are loud, you will not hear them. You will only hear the songs."

Nankya looked at her grandmother's face. In the morning light, the lines seemed less deep, the eyes brighter. There was something ancient in Naava's gaze, something that had seen too much and yet kept seeing.

"Will you sing with me?" Nankya asked.

Naava smiled. This time, the smile was full. "Yes," she said. "I will sing with you. But first, you must learn to listen."

She stood and walked to the eastern edge of the compound, where the baobab tree stood sentinel. Its trunk was massive, gnarled, older than memory. Naava placed her palm against the bark and closed her eyes.

"Listen," she said.

Nankya walked to her grandmother's side and placed her own hand on the tree. The bark was rough, warm from the rising sun. She closed her eyes.

"What am I listening for?"

"The tree," Naava said. "The tree is singing. Can you hear it?"

Nankya listened. At first, she heard only the wind, the distant birds, the low rumble of a truck on the road far away. But then, beneath those sounds, she heard something else. A vibration. A hum. It was faint, almost imperceptible, but it was there, rising up from the roots through the trunk and into her palm.

"I hear it," she whispered.

Naava opened her eyes. "Good," she said. "Now you are ready."

The sun climbed higher. The compound filled with the sounds of the day—the clatter of pots, the laughter of children, the lowing of cattle being led to pasture. But Nankya stood at the baobab with her hand on the bark, listening. And in her chest, the song took root.
